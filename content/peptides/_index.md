## What Are Peptides?

Peptides are short chains of amino acids — think of them as mini-proteins that act as signaling molecules in your body. They tell cells to do things: release growth hormone, regulate appetite, repair tissue, manage inflammation. Your body already makes hundreds of them naturally.

Therapeutic peptides are lab-made versions (or analogs) of these signals. Some you already know: **semaglutide and tirzepatide — the GLP-1 drugs — are peptides.** Others, like **sermorelin**, prompt your pituitary to release more of your own growth hormone rather than replacing it. And some popular "longevity" therapies, like **NAD+**, aren't technically peptides at all — NAD+ is a coenzyme — but they're usually offered on the same telehealth menus, so we cover them together here.

The appeal is precision: peptides target specific receptors and pathways instead of broadly flooding your system the way many traditional drugs do. The catch is that quality, dosing, and sourcing matter enormously — which brings us to the next point.

## Why Telehealth, Not DIY "Research Peptides"

If you've spent any time in peptide communities online, you've seen the gray-market route: vials of lyophilized powder sold "for research use only," which buyers reconstitute themselves with bacteriostatic water and dose with insulin syringes. We don't recommend it, and not for hand-wavy liability reasons:

<div class="callout callout--red">
<strong>The problems with self-sourced peptides:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>No identity or purity verification.</strong> Independent testing of gray-market peptides has repeatedly found underdosed vials, contaminated product, and in some cases entirely different compounds than what was on the label. You have no way to know what's in the vial.</li>
<li><strong>Reconstitution and dosing errors.</strong> You're doing mg-to-mL conversion math on a substance with no instructions. A misplaced decimal means a 10x overdose — this is one of the most common ways people get hurt with GLP-1 peptides specifically.</li>
<li><strong>Sterility risk.</strong> Home reconstitution isn't a sterile process. Injecting a non-sterile solution risks abscesses and serious infection.</li>
<li><strong>No medical oversight.</strong> Nobody screens your health history, checks interactions, adjusts your dose, or monitors side effects.</li>
<li><strong>"Research use only" means exactly that.</strong> These products are legally not for human use, and there's no recourse when something goes wrong.</li>
</ul>
</div>

Licensed telehealth platforms solve all of this: a clinician reviews your health history, the prescription is filled by a **regulated US compounding pharmacy** that tests for purity and potency, the medication arrives **pre-mixed and pre-measured** (no reconstitution, no dosing math), and you have a provider to contact if anything feels off. You'll pay somewhat more than gray-market prices — but you actually know what you're injecting.
